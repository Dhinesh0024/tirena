Every skincare product legally has to list its ingredients in one specific order — and once you know how to read it, you can tell more from the back of the bottle than from any marketing claim on the front.

### The rule that matters most

In the US, EU, and most other regulated markets, ingredients are listed in **descending order of concentration** — down to the 1% mark. After that point, brands can list remaining ingredients in any order they like. This single rule explains almost everything you need to know:

- **Positions 1–5** typically make up 80–95% of the entire formula. This is the actual product.
- Water (listed as **Aqua**) is the first ingredient in most skincare — that's normal, not a red flag.
- Anything you find in the last third of a long list is almost certainly present at well under 1%. It's there to appear on the label, not to meaningfully affect your skin.

### Why brands still name-drop that "hero ingredient"

This is where "fairy dusting" happens: a product markets itself around a trendy active — niacinamide, retinol, vitamin C — but the ingredient sits near the bottom of the list. It's technically true that it's "in there." It's also true that at that position, it's unlikely to be at an effective concentration. The label isn't lying to you; the front-of-box marketing is just telling a different story than the back-of-box list.

### A quick way to estimate concentration

If you know the exact percentage of one ingredient in a product (for example, a "2% Salicylic Acid" serum), everything listed *before* it is present at a higher concentration, and everything *after* it is lower. That single reference point can help you judge the entire rest of the list.

### What INCI names actually are

INCI (International Nomenclature of Cosmetic Ingredients) is the standardized naming system that makes labels comparable across brands and countries — it's why shea butter is listed as *Butyrospermum Parkii* and vitamin E as *Tocopherol*. Once you recognize a handful of these, most ingredient lists stop looking like a chemistry exam.

### Reading a label in three zones

1. **Top (positions 1–5):** the backbone of the formula — water, oils, or emollients
2. **Middle:** the active ingredients actually doing the work
3. **Bottom:** preservatives, fragrance, and trace ingredients under 1%

This isn't about memorizing every ingredient — it's about knowing where to look to tell a real formula from a marketing story.
