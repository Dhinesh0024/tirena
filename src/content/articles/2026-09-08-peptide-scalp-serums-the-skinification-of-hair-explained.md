"Skinification" is one of the biggest shifts in hair care this year — the idea that scalp deserves the same ingredient rigor as facial skin. Peptides, niacinamide, and hyaluronic acid, once reserved for serums and moisturizers, are now showing up in scalp treatments with the same scientific framing. It's a genuinely interesting shift — but it's worth understanding what the evidence actually supports before spending on it.

### What peptides are supposed to do

Peptides are short chains of amino acids that act as cellular signals. In skin, they prompt collagen production. Applied to the scalp, the idea is that they signal hair follicles to strengthen and extend their active growth phase. Common peptide actives you'll see listed include:

- **Redensyl** — aims to activate dormant hair follicles
- **Procapil** — targets follicle anchoring and strength
- **Anagain** — intended to extend the hair growth phase and reduce shedding
- **Capixyl** — often paired with the above for a broader multi-peptide approach

### The honest evidence picture

Here's the part most articles skip: most peptide scalp serum claims are borrowed from studies done on facial skin, not scalp-specific trials — the direct evidence for leave-in scalp serums is genuinely thinner than the marketing suggests. What does have solid, scalp-specific evidence behind it is simpler and free: **mechanical scalp massage alone has been shown to measurably improve hair thickness**, independent of any product at all.

That doesn't mean peptide serums are pointless — it means the honest framing is "a reasonable addition on top of a foundation that already works," not "the thing that will fix your hair on its own."

### How to actually use one

Apply to a clean, dry scalp before bed, focusing on thinning areas. Follow with 2-3 minutes of gentle scalp massage — this combines the one thing with strong direct evidence (massage) with the ingredient signaling the serum is designed to provide. Give it a full 8-12 weeks before judging results; peptides work through slow biological signaling, not immediate effect.
